# On Baptism Administered Less Than Lawfully

13 November 1561

This is a fresh English translation of the complete Latin advice printed in
*Ioannis Calvini opera quae supersunt omnia*, vol. X, part 1 (Corpus
Reformatorum XXXVIII), pp. 214–215. It was translated from the normalized
Latin and checked against the page images. It is not the translation by Mary
D. Beaty and Benjamin W. Farley in *Calvin's Ecclesiastical Advice* (1991),
and no wording from that copyrighted translation has been copied.

The CO editorial heading and bibliographical note are not translated because
they are editorial apparatus rather than Calvin's text. Square brackets mark
words supplied for intelligible English. The page marker at p. 215 is placed
before the English corresponding to *iteratam*; the printed page turn divides
that Latin word.

## Translation

### [CO X/1, p. 214]

Your letter was exceedingly welcome to us, excellent brothers, because at
last a place in the city has been granted to you and freedom to preach has
been given. God will crown this singular benefit with other and greater ones.
Although new and constant conflicts await you, and the devil's fury may
perhaps soon break out, you will have to press on courageously. We have not
answered your earlier letter before now because no one had presented himself
who could safely carry ours.

Concerning the question proposed, our judgment is as follows. Since no private
person is a lawful minister of baptism, this evil must be sternly resisted,
because it is a manifest profanation of baptism. And since this perverse
custom took its origin from superstition, when the necessity of salvation was
attached to the sign, this double evil is all the less to be fostered.
Augustine speaks hesitantly on this matter: “If any private person baptizes
when necessity compels, it is either no fault or a venial one.” Yet the
inviolable command of Christ should be worth far more to us: “Go, teach,
baptize” (Matthew 28:19). This connection cannot be dissolved without
sacrilege. To this is added the apostle's statement: “No one takes the honor
for himself, but he who is called” (Hebrews 5:4).

We therefore judge a baptism administered by a private man to be spurious. In
a properly ordered church this rashness would not be tolerable. But because
it happened among you at the beginning, before the order of the church had
been restored and while affairs were still confused, not only should the
error be pardoned, but baptism of whatever sort should be tolerated—provided
that what was once wrongly done is not drawn into a precedent. For amid the
church's dispersal God pardons many things that it would in no way be lawful
to admit into a well-ordered church. Of old, when religion had been corrupted,
circumcision was doubtless entangled in many faults and corruptions; yet we do
not read that it was

### [CO X/1, p. 215]

repeated when the people were recalled to pure worship. Therefore it is not
necessary—and indeed not expedient—to investigate every circumstance
anxiously, for that would beget countless scruples. Accordingly, let us too
bury what God pardoned under the papacy.

Now, when the church has been instructed about the true use of baptism,
whatever conflicts with Christ's institution should be regarded as nothing,
and anyone who has been polluted by a profane sprinkling should be baptized
afresh. As for baptism by women, our judgment is different. For since it was
monstrous audacity for women to thrust themselves into this office contrary
to the law of nature, we have no doubt that such an outrage should be rejected
without hesitation. Meanwhile there is no danger that weak consciences will
be wounded—unless someone pointlessly troubles himself through excessive
curiosity—because there is no one who knows that he was baptized by a woman.
Furthermore, because women were prohibited from the office of baptizing by an
ancient decree of the Council of Carthage, let no one take offense at the
novelty—a novelty that would have to be feared in cases involving men.

Farewell, excellent and most upright brothers. May the Lord govern you with
spiritual prudence, sustain you with strength, and enrich you with every gift.
We pray for abundant well-being for the elders and the whole church.

Geneva, 13 November 1561.

## Translator's notes

1. The heading *Ad baptismum minus legitime administratum* is supplied by the
   CO editors. “On Baptism Administered Less Than Lawfully” translates it for
   identification but is not presented as Calvin's own title.
2. “When the necessity of salvation was attached to the sign” describes the
   belief from which Calvin says emergency private baptism arose: necessity
   for salvation was ascribed to the baptismal sign itself.
3. “This connection” translates *hic nexus*, the linkage Calvin finds in
   Christ's command between teaching and baptizing. The referent is implicit
   rather than separately defined in the Latin.
4. “Spurious” translates *adulterinus*. The word can also mean illegitimate or
   counterfeit. The later sentences must remain with it: Calvin tolerates the
   past private-male baptisms in the church's unsettled beginnings and opposes
   making them a precedent.
5. “Dispersal” translates *dissipatio*. It can also convey disruption or
   disordered scattering; it is contrasted here with a well-ordered church.
6. “Profane sprinkling” renders *profana aspersio* literally. Calvin uses it
   for an administration after the church has been instructed that conflicts
   with Christ's institution.
7. “Without hesitation” translates *secure*: without scruple or fear of
   doing wrong, not physical safety.
8. The referent of *novitas* (“novelty”) is compressed and somewhat uncertain.
   In context it most likely means the new departure of rejecting—and thus
   repeating—a baptism administered by a woman. The translation retains the
   ambiguity rather than replacing the noun with that interpretation.
